\noindent\texttt{lc-visualizer} typesets \(\lambda\)-terms with support for coloring variables (bind-sensitive) and dynamic abstraction ranges. We also support reducing terms and converting them to their normal form step by step (if a normal-form exists), and to convert them to de Bruijn indices.
\begin{example}
\LC{(\\a.\\b.\a (\b \\b.\b \a)) a c}
\end{example}
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[textcolor]{(\\f.\\x.\f \x) \x}
\end{example}

\section{Writing terms}
|\LC{<term>}| typesets a term. It changes the category codes of |(|, |)|, |.| and |[|, so it only works in running text, not within the argument of another macro, a beamer frame without |[fragile]|, |align|, a footnote or a macro definition. Terms are not broken across lines. |\RLC{<term>}| works everywhere, but needs |\(|, |\)| and |\.| instead. In moving arguments like |\section| or |\caption|, give a short version for the table of contents (|\section[...]{...}|). The optional argument of both is the start of the color cycle (default~\(0\)) or |plain| for a black term without ranges.
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
   \toprule
   \textbf{Input} & \textbf{Meaning} & \textbf{Example} \\
   \midrule
   |\\x.<body>| & abstraction \(\lambda x.\,\mathit{body}\) & \LC{\\x.\x} \\
   |\\{x,y}.<body>| & several abstractions & \LC{\\{x,y}.\y \x} \\
   |\x| (|\a| to |\z|) & variable, colored like its abstraction & \LC{\x \y} \\
   |\\{foo}.<body>|, |\\{x'}.<body>| & abstraction over another name & \LC{\\{foo}.\lcVar{foo}} \\
   |\lcVar{foo}|, |\x'| & variable with another name & \LC{\\{x'}.\x' \lcVar{foo}} \\
   |\\[red]{x}[m]|, |\x[m]| & color of a binder, mark named |m| & \LC{\\[red]{x}[m].\x[m]} \\
   |(<term>)|, |\(<term>\)| & grouping & \LC{(\\x.\x) \y} \\
   |<term> <term>| & application (left-associative) & \LC{\f \x \y} \\
   |\Y|, |\equiv| & a bold Y and \(\equiv\) & \LC{\Y \equiv \Y} \\
   |yes|, |42| & plain words and numbers are single atoms & \LC{yes 42} \\
   |[<term>, <term>]| & a list of the \texttt{list} module & \LC[rule-depth=0]{[\a, \b]} \\
   \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The shortcuts |\a| to |\z| stand for the variables of those letters. A longer name is written |\\{name}.| as a binder and |\lcVar{name}| as a variable, and \cmd{lcEnableChar}\marg{name} adds a shortcut for it, so after |\lcEnableChar{foo}| the macro |\foo| is the variable |foo| as well.
\begin{example}
\LC{\\{fun,arg}.\lcVar{fun} \lcVar{arg}}

\lcEnableChar{fun}\lcEnableChar{arg}
\lcReduce{(\\{fun,arg}.\fun \arg) \lcVar{id} \lcVar{val}}
\end{example}
\begin{wideexample}
\RLC{\Y \equiv \\f\.\(\\x\.\f \(\x \x\)\) \(\\x\.\f \(\x \x\)\)}
\end{wideexample}
\begin{example}
\LC[3]{(\\a.\a) \\b.\b \a}

\LC[plain]{(\\a.\a) \\b.\b \a}
\end{example}

\section{Customization}
All settings are local to the current group. Every one of them has a macro and a key. \cmd{lcSetup}\marg{keys} sets the keys as the defaults for everything that follows, and |\LC[<keys>]|, |\RLC[<keys>]| and the reduction commands take the same keys for one term. A bare number stays the start of the color cycle, so |\LC[3]| and |\LC[color-start=3]| are the same. There is one key set, so the keys of the reductions (Section~\ref{sec:options}) work in \cmd{lcSetup} as well and \cmd{lcReduceSetup} is another name for it.
\begin{example}
\lcSetup{colors={teal,orange},lambda-ranges=false}
\LC{(\\x.\\y.\x) \y} \quad \LC[node-background=false]{(\\x.\\y.\x) \y}
\end{example}
A \emph{style} is a named group of settings. |style=<name>| applies it, \cmd{lcNewStyle}\marg{name}\marg{code} defines one, and the code is anything that could stand in \cmd{lcSetup}. The package brings |plain| (black, without ranges and shading) and |plain-math| (|plain| in the text font with a math~\(\lambda\)).
\begin{example}
\lcNewStyle{mine}{colors=red,font=\itshape}
\LC[style=plain]{(\\x.\x) \y} \quad
\LC[style=plain-math]{(\\x.\x) \y} \quad
\LC[style=mine]{(\\x.\x) \y}
\end{example}
\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{.58\linewidth}@{}}
   \toprule
   \textbf{Key} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
   \midrule
   \opt{style}, \opt{plain} & a named style, see \cmd{lcNewStyle} \\
   \opt{colors}, \opt{color-start}, \opt{color-by-name} & the color cycle, where it starts, and a color per name instead of per position \\
   \opt{node-background}, \opt{textcolor} & the TikZ node behind a variable, and the plain form without it \\
   \opt{text-mixin} & the color of a name that has no node \\
   \opt{color-mixin}, \opt{duplicate-mixin} & that node's fill and the shade of a repeated name \\
   \opt{shade-same} & shade a name that is bound again \\
   \opt{lambda}, \opt{font} & the \(\lambda\) symbol and the font of a term \\
   \opt{lambda-ranges}, \opt{paren-depth} & ranges of abstractions and of parentheses, both off by default \\
   \opt{glow}, \opt{single-lambdas} & glowing variables and one \(\lambda\) per binder \\
   \opt{rule-depth}, \opt{name-strut} & how deep rules are expanded and the strut of their labels \\
   \opt{inner-strut}, \opt{strut-line}, \opt{box} & space in rule boxes, line height, and the box around a term \\
   \opt{node-style}, \opt{bracket-color} & TikZ options of that node and the color of the parentheses \\
   \opt{arrow-from} & where an arrow leaves its term (|0| left, |.5| middle, |1| right) \\
   \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\keyentry{lambda-ranges}: draw the range of each abstraction (default |false|, as the ranges need room above and below a line).
\begin{example}
\lcSetup{lambda-ranges}
\LC{((\\a.\a) \b \a)}
\end{example}
\keyentry{paren-depth}: draw the ranges of parentheses up to that depth (default |0|).
\begin{example}
\lcSetup{paren-depth=2}
\LC{((\\a.\a) \b \a)}
\end{example}
\keyentry{colors}, \opt{color-start}, \opt{color-by-name}: the color cycle, where it starts, and whether every name keeps its own color. Reductions always color by name.
\begin{example}
\lcSetup{colors={orange,purple}}
\LC{((\\a.\a) \\b.\b \a)}
\end{example}
\keyentry{text-mixin}: how the name of a variable is colored without \opt{node-background}, |#1| is the color of its binder (default |#1!80!black|).
\begin{example}
\lcSetup{node-background=false, text-mixin=#1!45!black}
\LC{((\\a.\a) \\b.\b \a)}
\end{example}
\keyentry{node-background}: put every variable in a TikZ node with a colored background (default |true|), \opt{color-mixin} is the fill of that node, |#1| is the color. Without it a variable is only its name in its color, which \opt{text-mixin} sets. \opt{textcolor} is the other name of |node-background=false|, so |\LC[textcolor]| colors the names and draws no node.
\begin{example}
\lcSetup{color-mixin=#1!55!white}
\LC{((\\a.\a) \\b.\b \a)}

\lcSetup{textcolor}
\LC{((\\a.\a) \\b.\b \a)}
\end{example}
\keyentry{shade-same}: shade a variable whose name is bound again instead of giving it a new color (default |true|), \opt{duplicate-mixin} is that shade.
\begin{example}
\LC{(\\a.\a) (\\a.\a) (\\a.\a)}

\lcSetup{shade-same=false}
\LC{(\\a.\a) (\\a.\a) (\\a.\a)}

\lcSetup{shade-same, duplicate-mixin=#1!40!green}
\LC{(\\a.\a) (\\a.\a) (\\a.\a)}
\end{example}
\keyentry{glow}: color the text inside a variable node as well, so it glows. Pass a value to control how the text color mixes with the binding color (default |90|). It needs \opt{node-background}.
\begin{example}
\lcSetup{glow}
\LC{(\\a.\a (\\b.\b \c)) \a \c}
\end{example}
\keyentry{single-lambdas}: write a \(\lambda\) in front of every name of |\\{x,y}| (default |false|, where the names are separated by \opt{binder-gap}, the macro \cmd{lcSetBinderGap}). \opt{lambda} is the symbol itself and \opt{font} the font of a term.
\begin{example}
\lcSetup{single-lambdas}
\LC{\\{x,y}.\y \x}

\lcSetup{single-lambdas=false, lambda=\Lambda, font=\sffamily}
\LC{\\{x,y}.\y \x}
\end{example}
\keyentry{strut-line}: enlarge lines to fit the ranges, |true|, |false| or |auto| (the default), which enlarges them only while |lambda-ranges| is on. \opt{box}: whether |\LC| and |\RLC| typeset into an unbreakable box (default |true|).
\begin{example}
\lcSetup{box=false}
\LC{(\\a.\a) \b}
\end{example}
\keyentry{name-strut} puts its value into every rule label, and |name-strut| without one means |\strut|, which gives all of them the same height. \opt{inner-strut} is the space around the term in a rule box.

\cmd{lcBelowRewriteRule}\marg{name}\marg{term} puts a label below a term.
\begin{example}
\LC{\lcBelowRewriteRule{K}{\\{x,y}.\x} \\x.\x}

\lcSetup{name-strut={}}
\LC{\head \nil}

\lcSetup{name-strut}
\LC{\head \nil}
\end{example}
\keyentry{node-style}, \opt{bracket-color}: TikZ options of a variable node and the color of the parentheses.
\begin{example}
\lcSetup{node-style={draw=gray}, bracket-color=red}
\LC{(\\a.\a) \b}
\end{example}

\section{Marks}
|\mark{<name>}{<term>}| and |\\{x}[<name>]| name parts of a term. An arrow between two of them, also within TikZ pictures, is \cmd{reducemarks}\oarg{anchor}\marg{from}\marg{to}\oarg{TikZ options}. The arrow follows a flat curve over the terms. The style |lcbend| gives that path back to TikZ, so that |bend right| and friends work.
\begin{example}
\LC{(\\a[a].\a[a2] \b \c) \mark{b}{(\\b.\b)}}
\reducemarks{b}{a}[lcbend, bend right=10]
\reducemarks[south]{a}{a2}[green,-latex]
\end{example}

\section{Rewriting rules}
A rewriting rule is a named term. Rules are defined globally. The name of a rule must not use the rule's own macro. \cmd{lcCreateNewRewritingRule}\meta{cs}\marg{name}\marg{body} makes \meta{cs} available within terms. Single lowercase letters are the variables, so \meta{cs} needs a longer name. It is shown as a box with the name and the body. The starred form shows only the name and fits better into running text.
\begin{example}
\lcCreateNewRewritingRule\swap{swap}
      {\\{p,f}\.\p \false \true}
\LC{\swap \x} \quad \LC{\swap* \x}
\end{example}
\needspace{5\baselineskip}\cmd{lcCreateNewArgumentRewritingRule}\meta{cs}\marg{name}\marg{body} creates a rule with an argument, which is |\lcArg| in name and body. |\num| is defined as |\lcCreateNewArgumentRewritingRule\num{\lcArg}{...}| with a body that builds the numeral. Rule bodies are written like the argument of |\RLC|.
\cmd{lcRawArgumentRule}\meta{cs} then says that the argument of that rule is no single term but text the body reads itself; |\list| uses it, with |\lcListOf| as the body that turns |a, b, c| into the |\cons| chain.
Use it only for encodings whose shape depends on the argument, such as |\num{<n>}|, where every \(n\) gives a different term and \(n\) is a number, not a \(\lambda\)-term. A term as argument is never needed, as \(\beta\)-reduction inserts it. Write |\twice \f| with an ordinary rule instead, whose reduction also shows that step:
\begin{example}
\lcCreateNewRewritingRule\twice{twice}
      {\\{g,x}\.\g \(\g \x\)}
\LC{\twice \f} \quad \LC{\num{2}}
\end{example}
\begin{example}
\lcReduce{\twice \f}
\end{example}
\needspace{5\baselineskip}The key |rule-depth| sets how deeply nested rules are expanded (default~\(1\) in |\LC| and |\RLC|, |0| in reductions, where only the rule of the next \(\delta\)-step is expanded).
\begin{example}
\lcSetup{rule-depth=2}
\LC{\head \nil}
\end{example}

\subsection{Predefined rules}
The rules are grouped in modules. All of them are loaded unless you pass the package option |no-default-rewrites|. Then \cmd{lcLoadModule}\marg{modules} loads the given modules in the preamble, together with the modules they need. Rules exist only within |\LC|, |\RLC| and the reduction commands, so they do not clash with other packages, such as |\num| of \texttt{siunitx}.
\begin{code}
\usepackage[no-default-rewrites]{lc-visualizer}
\lcLoadModule{bool,list}
\end{code}
\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{.66\linewidth}@{}}
   \toprule
   \textbf{Module} & \textbf{Needs} & \textbf{Rules} \\
   \midrule
   \texttt{bool} & --- & |\true|, |\false|, |\ifelse|, |\and|, |\lor|, |\not| \\
   \texttt{pair} & \texttt{bool} & |\pair|, |\fst|, |\snd| \\
   \texttt{nat} & \texttt{bool} & |\num{<n>}|, |\succ|, |\pred|, |\add|, |\sub|, |\mult|, |\isZero|, |\eq| \\
   \texttt{comb} & --- & |\Ycomb|, |\Tcomb| \\
   \texttt{list} & \texttt{bool}, \texttt{nat}, \texttt{comb} & |\cons|, |\nil|, |\head|, |\tail|, |\ListNull|, |\length|, |\ListSum|, |\ListProd|, |\ListContains|, |\append|, |\list{<a, b>}| \\
   \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\needspace{8\baselineskip}
|\num{<n>}| is the Church numeral of \(n\), and |\Ycomb| and |\Tcomb| are fixed-point combinators for recursion. Lists are pairs ended by |\nil|. The recursive rules |\length|, |\ListSum|, |\ListProd|, |\ListContains| and |\append| use |\Ycomb|. The key |show-term| of the next section puts the term before its normal form, |fold=list| writes a list back as |[a, b]|. Every example below needs the \texttt{list} module, which pulls in \texttt{bool}, \texttt{nat} and \texttt{comb}.
The second element of a list, and the sum of one:
\begin{wideexample}
\lcNormalForm[show-term]{\head (\tail [\a, \b])} \quad
\lcNormalForm[show-term, fold=num, max-steps=500]{\ListSum [\num{2}, \num{1}]}
\end{wideexample}
The product of a list, and whether an element is in it:
\begin{wideexample}
\lcNormalForm[show-term, fold=num, max-steps=500]
   {\ListProd [\num{2}, \num{3}]} \quad
\lcNormalForm[show-term, fold={true, false}, max-steps=500]
   {\ListContains \num{1} [\num{1}]}
\end{wideexample}
A list can also be \emph{written} as |[a, b, c]|, which reads as |\cons \a (\cons \b (\cons \c \nil))|. It works in every command, in |\RLC| as |\[a, b\]| like its other syntax, and nests. An element made of several terms keeps them together, so |[a b, c]| is |\cons (\a \b) (\cons \c \nil)|. Where the brackets are read before the package sees them, as in a \texttt{beamer} frame or a footnote, the |\[a, b\]| form is the one that works. A mark follows its item directly, so |\x[m]| is the variable |x| with the mark |m| while |\lcVar{x} [a, b]| is |x| applied to a list. The rule |\list{a, b, c}| is the same list written as a rule: it shows as \LC[rule-depth=0]{\list{\a, \b, \c}} and unfolds into the |\cons| chain like every other rule. |\list{}| is the empty list, and a |\list| may stand inside another one.
\begin{wideexample}
\LC{\list{\a, \b, \c}} \quad
\lcNormalForm[fold=list, max-steps=500]{\tail (\list{\a, \b, \c})}
\end{wideexample}
With |fold=list| a list then goes in and comes out in that form:
\begin{wideexample}
\lcNormalForm[show-term, fold={list, num}, max-steps=500]
   {\append [\a] [\b]} \quad
\lcNormalForm[show-term, fold={list, num}, max-steps=500]
   {\tail [\a, \b]}
\end{wideexample}
\begin{wideexample}
\lcNormalForm[show-term, fold={list, num}, max-steps=800]
   {\ListSum [\num{2}, \num{3}]} \quad
\lcNormalForm[show-term, fold={list, num}, max-steps=800]
   {\length [\a, \b]}
\end{wideexample}
The length of two lists joined, and the factorial of~\(3\) through |\Ycomb|:
\begin{wideexample}
\lcNormalForm[show-term, fold=num, max-steps=500]
   {\length (\append [\a] [\b])}

\lcNormalForm[show-term, fold=num, max-steps=3000]
   {\Ycomb (\\{f,n}.\ifelse (\isZero \n) \num{1} (\mult \n (\f (\pred \n)))) \num{3}}
\end{wideexample}
Both combinators of \texttt{comb} compute fixed points, Curry's \(\mathbf{Y}\) and Turing's \(\Theta\). \(\Theta\,f\) reduces to \(f\,(\Theta\,f)\) with \(\beta\)-steps alone, so the recursion reappears unchanged, while \(\mathbf{Y}\,f\) reaches only the unfolded copy of itself.
\begin{example}
\LC[style=plain]{\Ycomb}\\
\LC[style=plain]{\Tcomb}
\end{example}
The same factorial through \(\Theta\):
\begin{wideexample}
\lcNormalForm[show-term, fold=num, max-steps=3000]
   {\Tcomb (\\{f,n}.\ifelse (\isZero \n) \num{1} (\mult \n (\f (\pred \n)))) \num{3}}
\end{wideexample}

\section{Reductions}
\subsection{Steps}
A reduction rewrites a term step by step until no step applies. The result is its \emph{normal form}, in which rules that are never applied to anything stay folded. \(M[x:=N]\) denotes \(M\) with the free occurrences of \(x\) replaced by~\(N\).
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{.32\linewidth}p{.5\linewidth}@{}}
   \toprule
   \textbf{Step} & \textbf{Rule} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
   \midrule
   \(\beta\)-reduction & \LC[plain]{(\\x.M) N} \(\to_\beta\) \(M[x:=N]\) & applies a function to its argument. In the row before the step, an arrow points from the argument to the binder \\
   \(\alpha\)-conversion & \LC[plain]{\\x.M} \(=_\alpha\) \LC[plain]{\\{x'}.M}, with \(x'\) for \(x\) in \(M\) & renames a bound variable, only when a \(\beta\)-reduction would otherwise capture a free variable of the argument \\
   \(\eta\)-conversion & \LC[plain]{\\x.M \x} \(\to_\eta\) \LC[plain]{M}, \(x\) not free in \(M\) & a function that only passes its argument on is that function \\
   \(\delta\)-step & \LC[plain]{c} \(\equiv_\delta\) \LC[plain]{body} & replaces a rewriting rule by its definition once it is applied to an argument. The row before shows the definition \\
   \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The steps are taken in normal order (leftmost-outermost), which finds the normal form whenever there is one. A rule behaves exactly like its body, so free variables in a body are never captured either. In \((\lambda x.\lambda y.x)\,y\), the argument \(y\) would end up under \(\lambda y\), so \(\lambda y\) becomes \(\lambda y'\) first:
\begin{example}
\lcReduce{(\\x.\\y.\x) \y}
\end{example}
\begin{example}
\lcReduce{\true \a \b}
\end{example}

\subsection{Semantics}\label{sec:semantics}
Terms are
\[ M, N \;::=\; x \;\mid\; \lambda x.M \;\mid\; M\,N \;\mid\; (M) \;\mid\; c \]
with variables~\(x\), parentheses that only group, and constants~\(c\). A constant is a rewriting rule or any other macro. Its unfolding \(\rho(c)\) is the body of the rule, for |\num{<n>}| the Church numeral, for a rule with a term argument~\(A\) its body with \(A\) substituted, and \(\rho(c) = \bot\) for a macro that is no rule.

The free variables \(\mathcal{F}(M)\) of a term are those that no binder of~\(M\) encloses. A constant stands for its unfolding, so it contributes the free variables of that:
\begin{align*}
   \mathcal{F}(x) &= \{x\} &
   \mathcal{F}(\lambda x.M) &= \mathcal{F}(M) \setminus \{x\} \\
   \mathcal{F}(M\,N) &= \mathcal{F}(M) \cup \mathcal{F}(N) &
   \mathcal{F}(c) &= \begin{cases} \mathcal{F}(\rho(c)) & \rho(c) \neq \bot \\ \emptyset & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\end{align*}
Substitution \(M[x:=N]\) is the usual one, with two details: it stops at a binder \(\lambda x\), and it enters a constant only for \(x \in \mathcal{F}(c)\), where \(c[x:=N] = \rho(c)[x:=N]\). Before it starts, \(\mathrm{ren}_{x,N}(M)\) renames every binder \(\lambda y\) with \(y \in \mathcal{F}(N)\) that has a free~\(x\) in its body to the first of \(y'\), \(y''\),~\dots\ that does not occur in the term. That renaming is the \(\alpha\)-step, after which the substitution captures nothing.

\small
A single step \(\mathrm{step}(M) = (k, M')\) with \(k \in \{\alpha, \beta, \eta, \delta\}\) takes the first case that applies, which is normal order. It is undefined if no case applies:
\begin{align*}
   \mathrm{step}(\lambda x.M\,x) &= (\eta, M) && \text{if } x \notin \mathcal{F}(M) \\
   \mathrm{step}(\lambda x.M) &= (k, \lambda x.M') && \text{if } \mathrm{step}(M) = (k, M') \\
   \mathrm{step}((\lambda x.M)\,N) &= \begin{cases} (\alpha, (\lambda x.\mathrm{ren}_{x,N}(M))\,N) & \mathrm{ren}_{x,N}(M) \neq M \\ (\beta, M[x:=N]) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \\
   \mathrm{step}(c\,N) &= (\delta, \rho(c)\,N) && \text{if } \rho(c) \neq \bot \\
   \mathrm{step}(M\,N) &= (k, M'\,N) && \text{if } \mathrm{step}(M) = (k, M') \\
   \mathrm{step}(M\,N) &= (k, M\,N') && \text{if } \mathrm{step}(N) = (k, N') \\
   \mathrm{step}(c) &= (\delta, \rho(c)) && \text{if } c \text{ takes an argument, } \mathrm{step}(\rho(c)) \text{ defined}
\end{align*}
\normalsize
The last case unfolds a rule with an argument when its body can still be reduced. Other rules stay folded until they are applied. A rule whose unfolding contains a substituted variable is replaced by its unfolding without a step of its own. With |eta=false| or |delta=false| the respective cases are left out.

\paragraph{Concrete semantics.} A trace \(\tau = M_0 \xrightarrow{k_1} M_1 \cdots \xrightarrow{k_j} M_j\) records the steps, with \(\mathrm{last}(\tau) = M_j\) and \(\lvert\tau\rvert = j\). For \(n\) = |max-steps|, the trace semantics of a term is the least fixpoint
\[
   [\![M]\!] = \mathrm{lfp}\,F_M, \qquad
   F_M(X) = \{M\} \cup \{\, \tau \xrightarrow{k} M' \mid \tau \in X,\ \lvert\tau\rvert < n,\ \mathrm{step}(\mathrm{last}(\tau)) = (k, M') \,\}
\]
on sets of traces ordered by inclusion. As \(\mathrm{step}\) is a function, \([\![M]\!]\) is the chain of prefixes of one trace. Its longest element \(\tau_M\) is what the package computes, step by step, as the Kleene iteration \(F_M^i(\emptyset)\).

\paragraph{Abstractions.} Every view is an abstraction \([\![M]\!]^\sharp = \alpha_\sharp([\![M]\!])\) of that trace:
\begin{itemize}
   \item \([\![M]\!]^{\mathrm{nf}} = \alpha_{\mathrm{nf}}(\tau_M)\) with \(\alpha_{\mathrm{nf}}(\tau) = \mathrm{last}(\tau)\) if \(\mathrm{step}(\mathrm{last}(\tau))\) is undefined and \(\bot\) otherwise, typeset as \(\mathrm{last}(\tau)\) followed by \(\cdots\). |\lcNormalForm| shows it, |\iflcNormalFormReached| tells whether it is not~\(\bot\).
   \item \([\![M]\!]^{\mathrm{rows}} = \alpha_{\mathrm{rows}}(\tau_M)\) keeps \(M_0\), \(M_j\) and every \(M_i\) that |hide|, |skip|, |compact|, |from| and |to| do not remove, and merges the steps in between. |\lcReduce| shows it.
   \item The relation of a merged segment is the join of its step kinds in the lattice \(k \sqsubseteq k^* \sqsubseteq {*}\) for each kind~\(k\), with \(k \sqcup k = k^*\) and \(k \sqcup k' = {*}\) for \(k \neq k'\). Hidden kinds are left out, and the join of nothing but hidden kinds is taken over all of them. These are the symbols \(\to_\beta\), \(\to_\beta^*\) and \(\to^*\).
   \item \([\![M]\!]^{\mathrm{fold}}\) abstracts the subterms of \([\![M]\!]^{\mathrm{nf}}\) by the rules of |fold|. A rule~\(c\) stands for \(\gamma(c) = \{ M \mid M =_\alpha \rho(c) \}\), and \(\alpha_{\mathrm{fold}}\) replaces a subterm \(M\) by the first listed \(c\) with \(M \in \gamma(c)\), for |num| by |\num{<n>}| if \(M\) is \(\alpha\)-equivalent to the Church numeral of~\(n\).
   \item With |tooltips|, a merged segment also carries its concretization, the skipped terms \(M_{i+1}, \dots, M_{l-1}\), as hover text.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Options}\label{sec:options}
The examples below use |\true| and |\false| of the \texttt{bool} module and |\num|, |\add| and |\succ| of \texttt{nat}, both loaded by default.
\keyentry{eta}, \opt{delta}: enable \(\eta\)-conversions and \(\delta\)-steps (default |true|).
\begin{example}
\lcNormalForm{\\x.\f \x}\\
\lcNormalForm[eta=false]{\\x.\f \x}
\end{example}
\begin{example}
\lcNormalForm{\true \a \b}\\
\lcNormalForm[delta=false]{\true \a \b}
\end{example}
\keyentry{max-steps}: stop after this many steps (default |50|). An unfinished reduction ends with~\(\cdots\). Recursive functions like |\length| need several hundred steps.
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[max-steps=2]
   {(\\x.\x \x) (\\x.\x \x)}
\end{example}
\keyentry{fold}: rules (by the name of their macro, with or without backslash, |num| for numerals, or |list| for the list notation of the \texttt{list} module, which also reads |[a, b]| as input) to write the result with. Every subterm that is \(\alpha\)-equivalent to one of them is replaced, and the first match wins, so |fold={false,num}| keeps \(\lambda x.\lambda y.y\) as |\false| instead of |0|.
\begin{example}
\lcNormalForm{\add \num{2} \num{3}} \quad
\lcNormalForm[fold=num]{\add \num{2} \num{3}}
\end{example}
\begin{example}
\lcNormalForm[fold={list, num}, max-steps=300]
   {\cons \num{1} (\cons \num{2} \nil)} \quad
\lcNormalForm[fold=list, max-steps=300]
   {\append (\cons \a \nil) (\cons \b \nil)}
\end{example}
\keyentry{layout}: |lines| (a table, default) or |inline|.
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[layout=inline]{(\\x.\\y.\x) \a \b}
\end{example}
\keyentry{hide}: kinds of steps whose resulting terms are not shown.
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[hide=alpha]{(\\x.\\y.\x) \y \a}
\end{example}
\keyentry{skip}: numbers or ranges (e.g.\ |skip={1,4-6}|) of the steps whose resulting terms are not shown.
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[skip={1, 2}]{(\\x.\\y.\x) \y \a}
\end{example}
\keyentry{compact}: show only the last of consecutive steps of the same kind. Hidden steps do not interrupt such a run. The relation of a row only names the kinds of steps that are not hidden.
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[compact]{(\\x.\\y.\x) \y \a}
\end{example}
\keyentry{from}, \opt{to}: do not show the terms before/after that step. The start term and the result are always shown.
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[from=2]{(\\x.\\y.\x) \y \a}
\end{example}
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[to=1]{(\\x.\\y.\x) \y \a}
\end{example}
\keyentry{alpha-symbol}, \opt{beta-symbol}, \opt{eta-symbol}, \opt{delta-symbol}: the arrow of each kind, as math. Several steps of one kind get a star.
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[beta-symbol=\rightarrow]
   {(\\x.\x) \y}
\end{example}
\keyentry{step-count}: write the number of merged steps next to the arrow instead of a star (default |true|). It counts only steps of that arrow's kind, so a kind |hide| removes does not count. \opt{step-mark} (\cmd{lcSetStepMark}) sets how that number looks, with |#1| and \cmd{lcSteps} for the number, which \opt{multi-symbol} can use as well; \cmd{lcStepMark} is the mark itself, the star when \opt{step-count} is off.
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[compact]{\true \a \b}

\lcReduce[compact, step-count=false]{\true \a \b}
\end{example}
\keyentry{step-mark-place}: |above| the arrow (default) or |behind| it, which keeps the row as low as a row without a mark.
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[compact,
   step-mark={\textbf{#1}}]{\true \a \b}

\lcReduce[compact,
   step-mark-place=behind]{\true \a \b}
\end{example}
\keyentry{multi-symbol}: relation for several steps of different kinds (default~\(\to^*\)). The kinds of the merged steps are |#1|, so they can be named.
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[multi-symbol=\Rightarrow, skip=1]{(\\x.\\y.\x) \y \a}

\lcReduce[multi-symbol={\overset{\text{#1}}{\to}}, skip=1]{(\\x.\\y.\x) \y \a}
\end{example}
\keyentry{arrows}, \opt{arrow-style}: draw the \(\beta\)-arrows (default |false|) and their TikZ options. An arrow points from the argument to the binder it is substituted for. Every variable needs a pair of marker nodes for that, which |arrows| switches on, so a reduction without them is the faster one.
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[arrows]{(\\x.\x) \y}
\end{example}
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[arrows, arrow-style={red, -stealth}]
   {(\\x.\x) \y}
\end{example}
\keyentry{marks}: give every variable those marker nodes without drawing arrows, so \cmd{reducemarks} can point at them.
\keyentry{underline}: underline what the next step reduces. It needs no nodes and no colors, so it also works with |style=plain| and in an |export|. \cmd{lcSetRedexLine}\marg{code} sets how that line looks, \cmd{lcRedexLine}\marg{term} draws one by hand.
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[underline]{(\\x.\\y.\x) \y \a}
\end{example}
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[underline, style=plain]{\true \a \b}
\end{example}
\keyentry{rule-depth}: how deeply rules are expanded when typeset (default |0|, only the rule of the next \(\delta\)-step is shown expanded).
\begin{example}
\lcNormalForm{\snd}\\
\lcNormalForm[rule-depth=1]{\snd}
\end{example}
\keyentry{color-start}: start of the color cycle.
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[color-start=3]{(\\x.\x) \y}
\end{example}
\keyentry{plain}: typeset without colors

\lcReduce[plain]{(\\x.\\y.\x) \y \a}

\begin{example}
\lcNormalForm[show-term]{(\\x.\\y.\x) \y \a}
\end{example}
\keyentry{format}: code to run before typesetting.

\end{example}

\keyentry{alpha-text}, \opt{beta-text}, \opt{eta-text}, \opt{delta-text}, \opt{lambda-text}: the relations and the \(\lambda\) in tooltips (PDF strings, e.g.\ |\lcUnichar{955}| for \(\lambda\)).

\subsection{Animations}\label{sec:animations}
Every shown term is a row, numbered from~\(1\). In beamer, with |beamer=uncover|, row~\(n\) appears on slide~\(n\). With |beamer=only|, it is shown only on slide~\(n\) (with |layout=inline| the terms stay in place). |beamer-start=<k>| moves the first row to slide~\(k\).
\begin{code}
\begin{frame}
  \lcReduce[beamer=uncover]{(\\f.\\x.\f \x) \x}
\end{frame}
\end{code}
|row-hook| is code with the row number as |#1| and the row itself as |#2|. With |layout=lines| it is called for the relation and for the term. It replaces a |beamer| setting and vice versa. Set it with \cmd{lcReduceSetup} outside of a beamer frame, as frames do not allow |#| in their body.
\begin{example}
\lcReduceSetup{row-hook=\fbox{#1: #2}}
\lcReduce[layout=inline]{(\\x.\x) \y}
\end{example}

\subsection{Caching and generated code}\label{sec:cache}
With |cache=<name>|, |\lcReduce| and |\lcNormalForm| write the code they typeset to |<name>.tex| (|cache| alone names it after the document and the hash, |\jobname-lc-<hash>.tex|). Its first line holds a hash of the term, the options and the rules. While it matches, later compilations input the file instead of reducing again. |\lcReduceSetup{cache}| caches every reduction. The file also restores the |\lcLast...| macros. It is \LaTeX{} code built from |\RLC| and internal macros of the package. A file that does not start with the hash line is never overwritten (this raises an error), reductions with errors are not cached, and the directory of |<name>| must exist. The cache saves the time of the reduction, not of typesetting it.
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[cache=reduce-k]{(\\x.\\y.\x) \a \b}
\end{example}
\keyentry{export}\texttt{=\meta{name}} writes the same reduction to \meta{name}|.tex| as an |array| of |\RLC| rows, without the internals of the cache. That file is meant to be read and changed: copy it into the document and edit the terms, the relations or the arrows. It uses only macros of the package: \cmd{lcStepState}\marg{steps}\marg{mark} hands the next arrow its number and its mark, and the arrow itself is \cmd{lcStepSymbol}\marg{relation}\marg{kind}\marg{mark}, or \cmd{lcMultiSymbol}\marg{kinds} when it merges several kinds. It is written on every run and never read back, and it carries no arrows, as those are pictures that a math |array| cannot hold.
\begin{example}
\lcReduce[export=\jobname-export]
   {(\\x.\\y.\x) \y \a}
\end{example}

\section{The reduction in the source}
Every listing in this appendix comes out of \texttt{lc-visualizer.sty} itself. The steps carry the names of Section~\ref{sec:semantics}.

A parsed term is a triple: a letter for the kind and two groups whose meaning follows from it.
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
   \toprule
   \textbf{Triple} & \textbf{Letter} & \textbf{Term} \\
   \midrule
   |{v}{<name>}{}| & \texttt{v}, variable & \(x\) \\
   |{l}{<name>}{<body>}| & \texttt{l}, lambda & \(\lambda x.M\) \\
   |{a}{<fun>}{<arg>}| & \texttt{a}, application & \(M\,N\) \\
   |{c}{<tokens>}{<unfolding>}| & \texttt{c}, constant & \(c\), unfolding \(\rho(c)\) \\
   \midrule
   |{B}{<abstraction>}{<arg>}| & \texttt{B}, marked \(\beta\)-redex & \((\lambda x.M)\,N\), the next step \\
   |{D}{<tokens>}{<unfolding>}| & \texttt{D}, marked \(\delta\)-redex & \(c\), the next step \\
   \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The last two letters live only between a step and its row and carry the part that the arrow and |underline| mark. Every walk over a term branches on the letter, so a term printer is four macros, one per letter; |\__lc_tp_l:nnn| prints an abstraction.

\paragraph{One step.} |\__lc_step_aux:nnn| is \(\mathrm{step}\). It clears |\g__lc_kind_str|, and a case that finds nothing leaves it empty, which is how the next case learns that it has to try.
\lcsource{step}
Under a binder, \(\eta\) comes before a step in the body.
\lcsource{lambda}
\(\eta\) needs the body to be an application of a variable that the body does not use otherwise, which is \(x \notin \mathcal{F}(M)\).
\lcsource{eta}
An application first tries itself, as a \(\beta\)- or a \(\delta\)-redex, then the function, then the argument. That order is what makes the whole thing normal order.
\lcsource{application}
\(\beta\) renames first. |\__lc_rename:nn| is \(\mathrm{ren}_{x,N}\) and reports through |\g__lc_renamed_bool| whether it had to touch anything: if it did, that renaming is the step, and the substitution waits for the next one.
\lcsource{beta}
The substitution itself is one macro per letter again. A binder of the same name ends it, a constant is entered only when the name occurs free in its unfolding.
\lcsource{subst}
\(\delta\) replaces a constant that stands in function position by its unfolding, and keeps the constant in the |{D}| triple so the row can still show it.
\lcsource{delta}

\paragraph{The whole reduction.} |\__lc_reduce:n| parses the term and applies \(\mathrm{step}\) until it is undefined or |max-steps| is reached. Every term it passes is stored, which is the trace \(\tau_M\).
\lcsource{loop}
The views read that trace: |\__lc_typeset_reduction:| keeps the rows that |hide|, |skip|, |compact|, |from| and |to| leave and merges the steps in between, |\__lc_typeset_symbol:n| turns the kinds of one merged segment into its relation, |\__lc_typeset_normal_form:| shows the last term alone.

\section{Benchmarking}
Reducing is cheap, typesetting is what costs, and every TikZ picture adds to it. \texttt{bench/run.sh} writes the table from the cases in \texttt{bench/cases.txt}. \texttt{bench/measure.tex} takes the times inside the document with the timer of pdf\TeX{}, so the start of \LaTeX{} and the shipout of the pages are no part of a number. Every case is measured five times, in a pdf\TeX{} run of its own, and \cmd{lcForgetMemo} drops what the last of those runs remembered, so that none of them is answered from the memory of the run before:
\begin{itemize}
   \item \textbf{Reduce} is |\lcComputeReduction[max-steps=<n>]|, which reduces the term and prints nothing.
   \item \textbf{Typeset} is |\lcReduce| with the same |max-steps| and the default keys, so it reduces \emph{and} shows every row. \textbf{Min} and \textbf{Max} are its fastest and its slowest of the five runs.
   \item \textbf{Cached} is the same |\lcReduce| with |cache|, the file already written, so the rows are read instead of computed.
\end{itemize}
